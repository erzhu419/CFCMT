\section{Introduction}

Traffic-signal controllers are commonly trained and tested within one fixed
network.  New random seeds change arrivals while leaving topology, routes,
phase programs and feature semantics largely unchanged.  Cross-network
deployment changes these conditions together.  A model can therefore fit
source transitions yet misrank feasible actions in a target network.
Action ordering, rather than transition accuracy alone, determines which
control decision the model recommends.

Two established approaches expose the challenge.  MaxPressure uses
local queue imbalance and has throughput and stability guarantees under its
stated store-and-forward assumptions \citep{varaiya2013maxpressure}.  The
PhasePressure comparator likewise uses local queue imbalance and
transfers without a city-indexed policy.  Pressure structure also underlies
controllers learned by reinforcement learning (RL),
such as PressLight and MPLight
\citep{wei2019presslight,chen2020thousandlights}.  The Flipping- and
Rotation-invariant All-Phase design (FRAP) represents competition
between conflicting phases from movement demand
\citep{zheng2019frap}.  These baselines are strong because their inputs
directly reflect the queues affected by signal decisions.

Dynamics-Aware Hybrid Offline-and-Online Reinforcement Learning (H2O) combines
fixed real-world data with interaction in an imperfect simulator
\citep{niu2022h2o}.  Its extension, An Improved Framework for Hybrid
Offline-and-Online Reinforcement Learning with Dynamics Gaps (H2O$^+$),
explicitly accounts for dynamics gaps \citep{niu2023h2oplus}.
Their real--simulator setting motivates our dense residual comparator,
although cross-network transfer introduces a different shift.  An
unrestricted residual can absorb network identity, demand scale and
phase-specific associations that change between cities.

Transfer-oriented signal control spans different target information budgets.
MetaLight adapts a meta-learned initialization with target experience
\citep{zang2020metalight}.  GEneral Scenario-Agnostic reinforcement learning
(GESA) normalizes heterogeneous intersections into unified state and action
structures \citep{jiang2024gesa}.  X-Light learns cross-city
decision-trajectory representations \citep{jiang2024xlight}, whereas
CrossLight combines source-city offline pretraining with target-city online fine-tuning
\citep{sun2024crosslight}.  Sim-to-real work such as Uncertainty-Aware Grounded
Action Transformation (UGAT) transforms actions to compensate for a
simulator dynamics gap \citep{da2023ugat}.  These methods instantiate distinct
approaches to topology normalization, meta-adaptation and dynamics-gap
correction.  Their zero-shot, online-interaction and sim-to-real protocols
answer different questions.  Our final comparison uses target offline
adaptation, with counterfactual simulator labels collected before deployment
and no target closed-loop reward used for model selection.

Causal representation work motivates modular mechanisms whose changes can
remain sparse across environments
\citep{bengio2019meta,scholkopf2021causal}.  Factored Adaptation for
Non-Stationary Reinforcement Learning operationalizes this idea through a
factored Markov decision process and individual latent change factors
\citep{feng2022factored}.  Our earlier mechanism-factored world model
restricted each predicted component to declared parents and adapted its
mechanism state separately.  Traffic control places a further requirement on
this structure.  Accurate decomposition of absolute dynamics does not
guarantee accurate ordering of feasible phases.  The final method retains
parent restrictions but changes the estimand from next-state prediction to
within-state action contrast.

We formulate Causal-Factored Cross-City Model Transfer (CFCMT) around matched
action contrasts.  At each simulator snapshot, every feasible phase is
executed from the same restored state.  Candidate costs are measured relative
to a source-selected pressure reference and normalized by the matched group's
action range.  A parent-restricted rigid anchor and an antisymmetric all-pair
model estimate these contrasts.  Their anchored interpolation
(\Cref{eq:anchored-blend}) is selected by out-of-fold (OOF) validation that
holds out complete target adaptation seeds.  A local confidence cap limits
the correction when the models disagree.  Definitions, formal statements and
inline proofs appear in
\Cref{sec:methods-matched-groups,sec:methods-pairwise,sec:methods-blend}.

The target information budget defines this adaptation setting.  CFCMT uses
target-network files, observed target state and counterfactual labels from an
uncalibrated Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO) model.  The simulator is not
fitted to field trajectories, but its counterfactual labels are target data.
The method is therefore calibration-light rather than zero-shot.  Adaptation,
offline confirmation and closed-loop seeds are disjoint.  The evaluation cache
was generated after the target model and selector were frozen.

Development uses 18 source networks from seven city groups, followed by
evaluation on two external city-derived networks, Los Angeles and Jinan.
In 56 matched 3,600-s rollouts, CFCMT is compared with fixed time,
simulator-only model predictive control (MPC), a rigid anchor, a dense
residual and two pressure policies.  All policies share target networks,
feasible phases, demand seeds, transition execution and metric populations.
An independent confirmation then pairs the unchanged CFCMT and dense models
on ten new seeds across the same four scenarios, adding 80 rollouts without
refitting or parameter selection.
Further controller comparisons distinguish source-plus-target control from
target-only control under fixed target adaptation budgets.  V91 evaluates
the original controller on 64 fresh seeds, four target scenarios and 512
rollouts.  V98 independently confirms a subsequent target-offline selector
on 56 further Jinan seeds.  Its topology-repaired refit and shared pressure
guard distinguish this procedure from the frozen V43 controller.  V-prefixed
identifiers denote frozen experimental protocols rather than method names.

The Reinforcement Signal Control (RESCO) benchmark
\citep{ault2021reinforcement} and LibSignal \citep{mei2022libsignal} RL runs
provide native-protocol integration checks.  Their state, reward, action
timing and training budgets differ from the paired experiment.
Same-protocol reproductions of CrossLight, X-Light and GESA remain a
comparison gap.

This study makes four contributions.  First, it formulates cross-network
signal adaptation around matched action contrasts and proves cancellation of
action-common domain bias (\Cref{prop:exact-cancellation}).  Second, it combines
a parent-restricted anchor with an antisymmetric all-action correction and
derives exact ranking and bounded perturbation properties
(\Cref{prop:pairwise-recovery,prop:blend-bounds,cor:bounded-local-perturbation}).
Third, it implements seed-blocked selection and full refitting, with OOF and
evaluation models excluded from deployment.  Fourth, it separates
structured-model gain, controller-pair performance and source-row attribution
through distinct frozen comparisons.  The central evidence concerns matched
action contrasts relative to a same-information dense residual and a
separately confirmed Jinan controller-pair gain.  Opposite V91 outcomes across
the two cities establish the need to retain network-dependent failures.
The V98 comparator's lower capacity limits source-row attribution, and
PhasePressure remains the stronger operational reference.
